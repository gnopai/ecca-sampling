Principles Material Functional Source Brand Size/Fragment Workflow
General Principles

Each item is examined individually. Record its characteristics in the applet, then set it aside and proceed to the next item. This approach lets you capture multiple attributes per item without large-scale physical sorting.

Categories cannot anticipate every type of trash found on the highway. Use the Notes field for anything unusual. Large debris that won't fit in bags may be documented separately with descriptive notes.

When in doubt, record what you can observe and add a note. An imperfect entry is better than a skipped item.
Material Categories

Material categories are mutually exclusive. Assign the primary material by volume. The first four categories (Paper/Cardboard, Plastic, Glass, Metal) correspond roughly to the 2022 statewide study and enable direct comparison.

  • Paper/Cardboard: Paper packaging, cardboard, napkins, paper cups, receipts. A paper cup with a thin plastic lining is still Paper/Cardboard — the plastic lining is not the primary material by volume.
  • Plastic: Bottles, containers, wrappers, bags, foam products. Foam is Plastic.
  • Glass: Bottles, fragments, and other glass materials. Fragments are Glass.
  • Metal: Aluminum cans, metal containers, other metallic debris.
  • Wood: Wood scraps, lumber pieces, pallet fragments.
  • Textile: Fabrics and clothing, including technical exercise gear made primarily of synthetic plastic fibers — classify by the item's function (clothing/textile), not by the polymer content.
  • Other: Genuinely mixed-material or unidentifiable items that do not clearly fit another category. Use sparingly.
A mylar balloon: the primary material is plastic film → Plastic. A paper bag with staples → Paper/Cardboard. A tire fragment → Other (rubber), or use the Automotive Debris functional category to flag it.
Functional Categories

Functional categories are overlapping — an item can belong to multiple categories simultaneously. Select all that apply.

  • Food Container: Food packaging, takeout containers, food wrappers — the broad catch-all. Use sub-options to specify further.
    • Takeout Container: Any container from a takeout or fast-food meal.
    • Beverage Container: Any container for a beverage.
      • Alcoholic: Beer cans, liquor bottles, wine bottles, and other alcohol-related packaging.
      • Recyclable: Metal cans or recyclable plastic bottles (check for the recycling symbol or obvious recyclability).
  • Toxic Fluid Container: Antifreeze, motor oil, weedkiller, and similar containers. Even empty containers count.
  • Automotive Debris: Vehicle parts, tire fragments, bumper pieces, and related materials.
  • Construction/Demolition Debris: Wood scraps, drywall, insulation, nails, concrete, cement.
  • Lawn/Garden Care: Lawn tools, fertilizer bags, pesticide containers, mulch packaging.
  • Marina-Related: Anything originating from or associated with the Washougal marina — rope, netting, buoys, boat parts.
  • Smoking Paraphernalia: Cigarette butts, vape waste, vape cartridges, lighters, cigarette packaging.
  • Attire: Clothing, gloves, shoes, hats, PPE items.
  • Furniture: Chairs, cushions, furniture fragments.
  • Lumber Tags: Identification tags that detach from lumber loads and blow onto the road. These are a specific concern for commercial hauler accountability.
A beer can → Food Container + Beverage Container + Alcoholic + Recyclable. A vape pen → Smoking Paraphernalia (material: Plastic or Other). A lumber tag → Lumber Tags (material: Paper/Cardboard or Other).
Likely Source

Likely source is a judgment call made by the sorter. This field is optional, and Unknown is a fully correct and acceptable answer — do not feel pressured to guess.

  • Intentional Litter: Items believed to have been deliberately discarded from a vehicle or by a pedestrian. Fast-food packaging, cigarette butts, and single-use drink containers are common examples.
  • Unsecured Loads: Materials believed to have fallen or blown from vehicle loads that were not properly secured. Construction debris, loose household items.
  • Commercial Haulers: Items that blew off secured commercial loads. Lumber tags are the archetypal example — they detach and blow off even from loads that appear properly secured. Plastic banding, packing film, and cardboard from pallets are other examples.
  • Unknown: Cannot determine the source with any reasonable confidence. Use freely — it is better to record Unknown than to record a guess as a fact.
"Unknown" is a data point, not a failure. The statewide study assigned likely source at the category level; we assign it per item, which means more unknowns are expected and appropriate.
Brand Information

Brand data helps assess corporate responsibility for highway pollution and is a key goal of this study. Record any identifiable brand — search the dropdown or type a brand name.

Add new brands as you encounter them using the "Other" entry. Brand attribution is ad-hoc and cumulative: ECCA will have complete freedom to document any brand encountered.

A crushed Modelo can where the logo is still readable → brand: Modelo. A generic plastic bag with no markings → leave brand blank. A faded fast-food cup where you can still read "McDonald's" → brand: McDonald's.

Only record a brand when you can confidently identify it. Degraded or ambiguous branding should be left blank rather than guessed.

Size & Fragmentation

Size is the approximate longest dimension of the item as found. Measure it as you find it — do not estimate what it would have been before it was broken or degraded.

  • Small: Less than 3 inches (roughly a cigarette butt, a bottle cap)
  • Medium: 3–6 inches (a standard soda can, a candy wrapper)
  • Large: 6 inches – 2 feet (a plastic bottle, a newspaper, a paper bag)
  • Very Large: Greater than 2 feet (a tire, a large piece of cardboard, furniture)

Fragment: Mark this if the item is clearly a piece of a larger broken item — scattered plastic shards, broken glass, torn sections of a bag. The fragment itself is measured as found, not reconstructed.

A plastic bottle cap: Small. Half of a glass bottle: Medium or Large depending on actual size — measure the fragment, not the original bottle. A tire fragment: depends on actual size of fragment found.
Workflow Overview

The applet is organized around a three-stage workflow for each bag:

  • 1. Bag tab: Enter transect ID, bag number, bag weight, and optionally your name and field notes. Save the bag record — this creates an active bag and takes you to the Items tab.
  • 2. Items tab: Enter one item at a time. After saving an item, you'll be asked if the bag has any more items. If yes, clear the form and continue entering items. If no, go to the Weights tab to record the plastic weight and confirm the bag.
  • 3. Weights tab: After all items in the bag are entered, record the plastic weight and press Confirm Bag. This marks all items as confirmed and returns you to the Bag tab for the next bag.

You cannot access the Items or Weights tabs without an active bag. The Review tab lets you return to any previous bag to edit items or re-confirm weights.

If you realize mid-way through a bag that you entered the wrong bag number, go to the Bag tab and change the bag number — the app will offer to update all items already recorded for that bag.
